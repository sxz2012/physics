1 Introduction: An Ancient Sprinter
In 2003, Mary Pappen Jr., a young Mutthi Mutthi Aboriginal Australian girl, discovered footprints in the ground of an
old, dried-up lakebed.
A scientist named Steve Webb studied the footprints. He claimed some of them were made by a person running
barefoot through the mud as fast as a modern day sprinter in the Olympics. News outlets picked up the story, and
soon people were talking about the ancient sprinter who could rival today’s best. However, a few years later, the
story was essentially forgotten.
How did Webb estimate the speed of this ancient sprinter? How reliable was his work, and why did the story seem
to die away in just a few years? That’s what we’ll examine in this handout as we learn about speed and
motion.
2 From Footprints to Dot Diagrams
After Pappen’s discovery, people studied the footprints in different ways. For example, Webb used a high-tech
method called ”optically stimulated luminescence” to estimate that the footprints were between 19,000 and 23,000
years old. This method uses specialized, sensitive equipment and is based on the theories of radioactive decay and
how electrons move in the small grains of minerals found in dirt.
A group of Pintubi Aboriginal Australian trackers came to study the footprints as well. Using their lifelong
experience with human footprints, they realized some of the tracks were made by families walking, but a few were
made by a group of hunters chasing an animal. One hunter in particular was running fast to cut off the animal’s
escape. This hunter was the ancient sprinter from Webb’s report.
To estimate how fast the hunter was running, Webb used neither high tech equipment nor expert judgment.
Instead, he made measurements with a tape measure and then used a conceptual model. A conceptual model is a
simpliﬁed way of thinking about a speciﬁc part of the world. Conceptual models can combine measurements,
equations, diagrams or pictures, graphs, and even computer programs to explain something. They are never
perfect, but they can still be good enough to help us learn new things.

This sort of picture, using dots spaced an equal time apart to represent motion, is called a dot diagram. It’s very
simpliﬁed, but it represents the core information: the stride length.
A dot diagram can model anything that moves. For example, imagine a car dripping a drop of wet paint every 0.6 s.
The dots of paint on the road would form a dot diagram.
There is no need for the dots to be drawn every 0.6 s. Within a dot diagram, we need the same time between all the
dots, but that time can be any amount of time we wish. However, if we want to compare two different dot diagrams,
they should have the same time between dots.
Additionally, we can use dot diagrams even when there are no actual dots. We can take any object and imagine it
leaving dots behind as it moves.

4 A model of stride time
Although Webb had to estimate the time between strides, he didn’t have to guess blindly.
Imagine a child and an adult walking together. The child is short, so to keep up with the adult, they have to take
more steps than the adult, and the time between their steps will be smaller than for the adult. This suggests that
height affects time between strides.
Webb didn’t know how tall the ancient hunter was, but he did have one useful piece of information: the foot length.
Tall people tend to have longer feet, so Webb could use the foot length to guess the hunter’s height. Then he could
combine the height and stride length to guess the time between the hunter’s strides, and ﬁnally use stride length
and time between strides to calculate the speed.
Webb didn’t do all that work on his own. Instead, he relied on a model built decades earlier by three Canadian
scientists. The Canadian team used data on modern day humans to create a series of equations that take as inputs
the stride length and foot length and give an estimated time between strides as output. These equations, along
with some plots they made of the same information, formed their model of human movement.
When Webb used the Canadian scientists’ equations, he found a time of 0.360 s between strides for the hunter. He
also measured a stride length of 3.73 m. Combining these, he found a speed of 3.73 m
0.360 s = 10.36 m/s.

5 Questioning a model
If we want to know whether the model Webb used gives reasonable results, we could look at some of its claims
besides the speed of the hunter, and see how reasonable they are.
When we do this, things don’t look great for the model. Usain Bolt’s average stride length in his world record was
4.88 m, much longer than the 3.73 m stride length of the ancient hunter. With only that information, it would make
sense to guess that Bolt was running signiﬁcantly faster. It’s only because the model predicted an extremely short
time between strides for the hunter (0.360 s for the hunter as opposed to 0.467 s for Bolt) that the hunter came out
around the same speed.
Perhaps that’s because the hunter was much shorter than Bolt and short people take less time between strides?
Usain Bolt is 195 cm tall, and the model predicted that the hunter was 194 cm tall - essentially the same
height.
Webb applied the same model to several other tracks near the site. None of them came out to have running speeds
as fast as the hunter, but there were many other unusual numbers, including many instances of very short times
between the strides.
When we question our models and test how reasonable their outputs are, we’re doing a sanity check. In this case,
the model Webb used seems a bit unbelievable, and doesn’t quite pass our sanity checks.

The equations Webb used were made to model humans walking, not running. When Webb took the equations from
the Canadians’ model and applied them to the hunter, he was able to calculate something, but the calculation
wasn’t meaningful. It used inputs from beyond the region where the model was intended to work.
If Steve Webb had understood the Canadians’ model better before using it, he could have realized the model wasn’t
appropriate for estimating the speed of the hunter. Or, after using the model, he could have done some sanity
checks and noticed that the results were unrealistic. He could also have asked a colleague with more experience in
using these models for help. Unfortunately, neither Steve Webb, nor any of the scientists who reviewed his work,
noticed the mistake.
From there, the inappropriately calculated speed spread to news stories and books. Journalists and authors
repeated the ﬁgure without doing a critical check on where it came from.
In 2013, Javier Ruiz and Ang´elica Torices, two Spanish biologists and paleontologists, re-analyzed the hunter’s
footprints using a new model that was designed for running speeds. Their model and was tested against real data
from people running. Ruiz and Torices estimated a running speed of 7.2 ± 1.0 m/s for the hunter. This means that
they couldn’t tell exactly how fast he was going, but estimated that his speed was probably between 6.2 m/s and
8.2 m/s. The hunter, while running quite fast, was not running nearly as fast a modern Olympic sprinters.
This is why when we generate models, we need to test them against real data to determine their reliability. We also
want to remember the limitations of our models and do sanity checks on our results. Finally, we should report our
results with uncertainty whenever we can to indicate just how accurate we think our ﬁndings are.

6 Conclusions
Here, we’ve looked at one attempt to model speed. Understanding this model led us to an equation for speed,
distance, and time, and to the notion of dot diagrams. As we continue solving problems and building new models in
class, we’ll introduce new technical concepts to deal with those problems and models, including new mathematical
notation, creating and analyzing graphs, and distinguishing closely related concepts such as speed and
